# 90-Day Foundations Plan: Programming, Maths, Data Science & AI

Oct 17, 2026 · @Prateek

## Overview

In 90 days, from Monday 19 October 2026 to Saturday 16 January 2027, you go from no programming and rusty school maths to a solid base for any data science and AI degree. The plan assumes about 12 hours a week across four days (Monday, Friday, Saturday and Sunday) and is built around practical problems, many drawn from your manufacturing and EdTech experience.

Four tracks run in parallel every week:

- **Python programming** (about 4.5 hrs/week, including the weekend project): from first lines of code to cleaning and analysing real datasets with pandas.
- **Mathematics** (about 3.5 hrs/week): rebuilt from arithmetic and algebra up to functions and a first look at linear algebra.
- **Statistics and data science** (about 2 hrs/week, plus the weekend project): descriptive statistics, probability, distributions, correlation, and data analysis.
- **AI landscape** (about 1 hr/week): one concept and one current development each week, so you understand the field, not just the headlines.

The 13 weeks fall into three phases:

| Phase | Weeks | Dates | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Diagnostic | Day 0 | 18 Oct | 30-minute maths check to find your real starting point |
| 1. Rebuild | 1–4 | 19 Oct – 15 Nov | Arithmetic and algebra, Python basics, describing data |
| 2. Build the core | 5–9 | 16 Nov – 20 Dec | Functions (given extra time), probability, Python data structures, pandas |
| 3. Consolidate and apply | 10–13 | 21 Dec – 16 Jan | Buffer week, matrices, capstone, entrance-style practice |

The plan is programme-neutral. It covers what most online data science degrees and their entrance or qualifier tests expect: school-level maths, introductory statistics, logical problem-solving, and basic programming.

**Built for a fresh start.** The plan assumes no programming experience and a 20-year gap since studying maths. Three safeguards keep the pace realistic:

- **Day 0 diagnostic:** before week 1, a short maths check with Claude shows what has come back and what needs rebuilding, so familiar topics go faster and shaky ones get more time.
- **Extra time on algebra and functions:** these underpin almost all degree-level maths, so each gets more than one week.
- **Week 10 is a buffer week:** it falls over the holidays and is reserved for catching up and revising, with no new topics.

Topics marked **(stretch)** are worth previewing but not essential before a degree, which will teach them properly. If time runs short, skip them without guilt.

## Weekly rhythm

Two hours on Monday and Friday plus four hours each on Saturday and Sunday add up to 12 hours. Monday and Friday each introduce one new idea; the weekend is for practice, data work and review.

| Day | Time | Main session | Short add-on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | 2 hrs | Maths: new concept + problems | 10 min Python recall |
| Tuesday–Thursday | Optional 10–15 min a day | Flashcards (Anki) to keep the week's ideas warm | — |
| Friday | 2 hrs | Python: new concept + exercises | 10 min maths recall |
| Saturday | 4 hrs | Maths practice set (1.5 hrs), Python exercises + one mini-program (1.5 hrs), statistics and data science (1 hr) | Logic puzzle or algorithm tracing |
| Sunday | 4 hrs | Weekend project (2 hrs), AI landscape (1 hr), review quiz and weak spots (45 min) | Plan next week (15 min) |

With three days off midweek, the optional flashcards matter: short daily recall stops Monday's maths fading by Friday. On four-hour days, work in 50-minute blocks with 10-minute breaks.

Each session follows the same four steps:

1. **Problem first** (10 min): start with a practical question you can't yet answer, such as "Which shift produces the most defects?"
2. **Learn the concept** (30–40 min): only what the problem needs, with an example.
3. **Practise** (50–60 min): problems that get harder, written by you, not copied.
4. **Explain it back** (10 min): write three lines in your own words in a learning journal. If you can't explain it simply, revisit it.

If a week runs short of time, protect the maths and Python sessions first; the landscape track can shrink to 30 minutes.

## Milestones and self-checks

At the end of each phase, tick these off honestly. Anything unticked becomes the first task of the next phase.

**Day 30 (15 November): Rebuild complete**

- [ ] I can solve linear equations and word problems without a calculator
- [ ] I can find the slope and intercept of a line and sketch it
- [ ] I can write a Python program using variables, conditions and loops without looking up the syntax
- [ ] I can read a Python error message and fix the cause
- [ ] I can calculate mean, median and standard deviation and explain what each tells me
- [ ] I can explain the difference between AI, machine learning and generative AI to a friend

**Day 60 (15 December): Core built**

- [ ] I can sketch linear, quadratic, exponential and log functions and describe their shapes
- [ ] I can solve basic probability and conditional probability problems
- [ ] I can write my own functions and use lists and dictionaries comfortably
- [ ] I can trace an algorithm step by step on paper and predict its output
- [ ] I can read a CSV file in Python and summarise its columns
- [ ] I can explain the normal distribution and what a z-score means

**Day 90 (16 January): Ready for a degree**

- [ ] I can explain what a matrix multiplication does (stretch: and what a derivative represents)
- [ ] I can group, merge and visualise a dataset and describe what I found
- [ ] I can explain a correlation and a simple regression line in plain words (stretch: train and evaluate a simple model)
- [ ] I scored 70% or more on a full maths and statistics mock test
- [ ] My capstone is on GitHub with a one-page summary
- [ ] I can talk for five minutes about where AI is heading and why

## AI landscape track

The goal is understanding, not keeping up with every headline: one concept and one current development a week, always linked back to what you're learning. The weekly topics are in the chart above; this is how to run the track.

**The weekly hour (Sunday)**

1. **Concept (20 min):** learn the week's idea, such as embeddings or agents, at a plain-language level.
2. **Current development (20 min):** find one recent piece of news on that theme. Ask Claude for a short briefing with web search, then read one original source.
3. **Connect (15 min):** ask "Which maths, statistics or Python idea from my plan sits underneath this?"
4. **Journal (5 min):** three lines: what happened, why it matters, and whether it's a real shift or hype.

**Three questions to ask of any AI announcement**

- What can it actually do that wasn't possible before, and what's the evidence?
- What data and compute does it depend on, and who controls them?
- Who benefits, who might be harmed, and what could go wrong?

**Sources worth following**

- **Weekly newsletters:** *The Batch* (DeepLearning.AI) for plain-language summaries; *Import AI* for deeper research trends.
- **Annual big picture:** the Stanford *AI Index Report*, which tracks progress, investment and policy with data.
- **Primary sources:** research and product blogs from the major AI labs, read when a story matters to you.
- **Indian context:** national AI policy news and the AI programmes of Indian IT and EdTech companies, relevant to your career.

By week 13 you'll have about a dozen journal entries. Turning them into a one-page "AI landscape map" is the final task for this track.

Pick one capstone in week 11 and build it in weeks 12 and 13. Both use public data only, never employer or learner data.

| Your edge | Your EdTech operations experience | Your process excellence experience |

Whichever you choose, the one-page summary matters as much as the code: state the question, what you found, how confident you are, and what action you'd recommend.

## Learning well with AI and staying on track

AI makes learning faster only if you do the thinking first. These rules keep it that way.

**Using AI as a tutor**

- **Try first, ask second.** Spend at least 15 minutes on a problem before asking for help, and then ask for a hint, not the answer.
- **Never paste code you can't explain.** If Claude writes something, retype it and explain each line back.
- **Ask to be tested.** End each week with a quiz on that week's topics, and redo any question you got wrong three days later.
- **Check, don't trust.** Verify important facts and calculations; AI can be confidently wrong.

**Staying on track**

- **Same slots, every week.** A fixed schedule beats motivation. Keep your Monday, Friday and weekend sessions at consistent times so the habit becomes automatic.
- **Never miss twice.** A missed session is normal; two in a row starts a slide.
- **Track visibly.** Tick the milestones above and keep the learning journal going.
- **Expect the dip.** Weeks 5–7 usually feel hardest, as new ideas pile up before they connect. That's a sign of progress, not failure.
- **Review weekly.** Every Sunday, spend 15 minutes asking: what clicked, what didn't, and what changes next week?
